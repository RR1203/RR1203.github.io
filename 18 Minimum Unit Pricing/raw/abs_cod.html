Australia's leading causes of death

There were 188,421 deaths registered in Australia in 2025, an increase of 1,153 deaths from 2024. When adjusted for age-structure and population size, the age-standardised mortality rate decreased to 495.5 deaths per 100,000 people, from 507.8 the previous year. Relatively lower mortality rates in 2025 indicate a return towards pre-pandemic trends. 

The ABS is planning to publish a paper in the first half of 2027 examining mortality trends over time, including how methods for measuring these trends have evolved to assess the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on mortality indicators.

Identifying and comparing leading causes of death in populations is useful for tracking changes in patterns of mortality and identifying emerging trends. The leading cause groups have been reviewed and updated in 2025. For more information related to the tabulation of leading causes, refer to Processing the data – Methods for cause of death analysis in the methodology.

In 2025:

  • The leading cause of death was dementia, including Alzheimer's disease, accounting for 10.1% of deaths. 2025 is the first year that dementia has accounted for over 10% of all deaths in Australia.
  • Ischaemic heart diseases were the second leading cause of death and accounted for 8.6% of deaths.
  • Since dementia overtook ischaemic heart diseases as Australia’s leading cause of death, the gap between these two causes has widened as deaths due to dementia continue to increase over time and deaths due to ischaemic heart diseases decrease. In 2025, there were 2,791 deaths separating the top two leading causes.
  • The mortality rate for ischaemic heart diseases was the lowest on record since its peak in 1968.
  • Cerebrovascular diseases, lung cancer and chronic lower respiratory diseases rounded out the top five leading causes.
  • Influenza and pneumonia was the 11th leading cause of death and accounted for 2.3% of all deaths. The last time over 2% of all deaths were caused by influenza and pneumonia was in 2019.
  • Suicide was the 15th leading cause of death and the leading cause of premature death. People who died by suicide had a median age at death of 45.0 years.
  • The top five leading causes accounted for one-third (33.3%) of all registered deaths.

Between 2016 and 2025:

  • The number of deaths due to dementia increased by 37.3%.
  • The number of deaths due to ischaemic heart diseases and cerebrovascular diseases decreased by 16.2% and 12.1%, respectively.
  • The number of deaths from accidental falls increased by 59.5% over the period. Accidental falls were the seventh leading cause of death in 2025, compared to the ninth a decade ago. They were the highest ranked external cause of death. Accidental falls have a high median age at death at 87.5 years. Common complications from falls leading to death include hip fractures, immobility and pneumonia.

For dementia information and support please contact the National Dementia Helpline on 1800 100 500.

Age-standardised death rates

Age-standardised death rates (SDRs) enable the comparison of mortality patterns over time as they account for changes in the size and age structure of the population. For more information on age-standardised death rates, refer to Processing the data – Methods for cause of death analysis in the methodology.

Between 2016 and 2025:

  • The age-standardised death rate for ischaemic heart diseases decreased to its lowest since official cause of death statistics began collection in 1968 (42.0 deaths per 100,000 people in 2025).
  • While the number of deaths due to dementia increased over the 10-year period, the age-standardised death rate for dementia has been more stable, reflecting an ageing population.
  • The rate of deaths due to cerebrovascular diseases decreased by 31.6%.
  • While deaths due to lung cancer and chronic lower respiratory diseases both increased in number, the standardised death rate for these causes decreased by 17.3% and 14.6%, respectively.

Years of potential life lost

Years of potential life lost (YPLL) is a measure of premature mortality which weights age at death to gain an estimate of how many years a person would have lived had they not died prematurely. Causes of death with a younger median age at death will have a higher number of YPLL. The ABS methodology defines a "premature death" as one where the person was aged between 1-78 years at the time of death. For more information on years of potential life lost, refer to Processing the data – Methods for cause of death analysis in the methodology.

In 2025:

  • Suicide was the leading cause of premature death, accounting for the highest number of potential years of life lost (108,674). People who died by suicide had a median age at death of 45.0 years.
  • Ischaemic heart diseases had the highest number of deaths occurring in those aged under 79, but the second highest number of YPLL at 72,039 years. People who died from ischaemic heart diseases had a median age at death of 82.6 years.
  • People who died from accidental drug overdose, lung cancer and land transport accidents had the third, fourth and fifth highest YPLL, with median ages of 47.3, 75.4 and 49.5 years, respectively.

Leading causes of death by sex - Males

For the 98,762 males who died in 2025:

  • Ischaemic heart diseases were the leading cause of death (10,263 deaths).
  • While the death rate for ischemic heart diseases remains considerably higher than the second ranked cause (dementia at 7,391 deaths), the rate ratio has narrowed over time. Ten years ago, the mortality rate for ischaemic heart diseases was 2.1 times higher than that of dementia. In 2025, the rate for ischaemic heart diseases was 1.4 times higher than that of dementia.
  • Four of the top 10 leading causes of death in males were malignant neoplasms (cancer), specifically lung cancer, prostate cancer, blood and lymph cancer and colorectal cancer. These cancer types have consistently been the top four leading causes of cancer deaths in males. Overall, cancer accounted for 29.8% of all male deaths in 2025.
  • Suicide remained the 11th leading cause, and the second highest ranked external cause of death in males. 73.9% of people who died by suicide were male.

For males from 2016 to 2025:

  • The overall mortality rate decreased by 9.2% to 581.3 deaths per 100,000 people, which is the lowest mortality rate on record for males in Australia.
  • The death rate for ischaemic heart diseases decreased by 29.9%.
  • The death rate for dementia, including Alzheimer's disease increased by 4.3%.

Leading causes of death by sex - Females

For the 89,659 females who died in 2025:

  • Dementia, including Alzheimer's disease was the leading cause of death (11,628 deaths), with close to two-thirds (61.1%) of people dying from the disease being female. Dementia has been the leading cause of death for females since 2016.
  • Ischaemic heart diseases were the second leading cause with 5,965 deaths.
  • Accidental falls were the eighth leading cause of death and the leading external cause of death.
  • Breast cancer was the sixth leading cause overall and second leading cause of cancer deaths with 3,244 deaths.

For females from 2016 to 2025:

  • The overall mortality rate decreased by 6.9%.
  • The death rate for dementia increased by 7.0%.
  • The death rate for ischaemic heart diseases decreased by 41.1%.

Recent years have seen a change in the leading causes of premature mortality for females.

  • Until 2020, breast cancer was the leading cause of premature mortality, but was surpassed by suicide in 2021 to 2023, and now 2025.
  • In 2025, breast cancer accounted for 25,503 years of potential life lost for females.
  • Suicide accounted for 30,145 years of potential life lost. This is the highest in the time series and the first time years of potential life lost due to suicide has exceeded 30,000 years for females.

Leading causes of death by state and territory

Despite differences in population size and demographics, the leading causes of death are now highly similar across most states and territories, with the Northern Territory the main exception.

In all jurisdictions except the Northern Territory:

  • Dementia was the leading cause of death and ischaemic heart diseases were the second leading cause of death.
  • The third to fifth leading causes of death were cerebrovascular diseases, lung cancer, and chronic lower respiratory diseases, with some differences in rankings.

In the Northern Territory, diabetes and ischaemic heart diseases were equally ranked as the leading causes of death. Diabetes was the cause of death for 9.5% of all deaths in the Northern Territory, compared with 3.1% nationally. 

Over the past 20 years, all of Australia’s states and territories have seen an increase in causes of death that primarily affect older Australians, notably dementia and accidental falls.

  • In 2006, dementia accounted for between 2.3% and 5.6% of deaths in each jurisdiction. By 2025, this increased to between 4.1% and 12.1% of all deaths per jurisdiction.
  • Accidental falls accounted for between 0.6% and 1.6% of deaths per jurisdiction in 2006, and between 1.9% and 3.8% in 2025.

Leading causes of death - historical trends

The table below presents a time series of leading causes of death for selected years from 1968 to 2025. 

  • Between 1968 and 1978, ischaemic heart diseases accounted for 30% of all deaths. In 2025, 8.6% of all deaths were caused by ischaemic heart diseases.
  • In 1968, dementia, including Alzheimer's disease accounted for 0.2% of all deaths, compared with 10.1% in 2025. Dementia first appeared in the top 5 leading causes of death in 2006.
  • Influenza and pneumonia last appeared in the top 5 leading causes of death in 1970. While the number of deaths has remained relatively stable across years, their relative contribution to all deaths has decreased due to Australia’s growing population and improvements in medical and health care. In more recent years, the ranking of influenza and pneumonia can be dependent on the severity of an influenza season.
  • Land transport accidents consistently ranked in the top 5 leading causes from 1968 until 1978. In 2025, land transport accidents were the 35th ranked leading cause of death.
  • Since the 1970's, deaths due to lung cancer have consistently accounted for 4% to 6% of deaths each year. In 2025, 4.8% of deaths were caused by lung cancer.

Median age at death in Australia

In 2025, dementia, including Alzheimer's disease was Australia's leading cause of death. While this finding helps explain what Australians are dying from, it does not tell us when those deaths occur. Looking at median age at death provides a different perspective on mortality, revealing whether Australians are dying later in life, how age at death differs between causes, and which causes contribute most to deaths occurring at younger ages.

The median age at death is the age at which half of all deaths occur at a younger age and half occur at an older age. As a measure, it complements traditional mortality statistics by focusing on the timing of death across the life course rather than the number of deaths alone.

Australia's population has aged substantially over recent decades, and deaths are increasingly concentrated at older ages. In 2025, the median age at death was 81.9 years, and more than two-thirds (68.4%) of deaths occurred among people aged 75 years and over. Understanding how age at death differs by cause of death, sex and geographic area can help explain broader changes in Australia's mortality profile.

In the 25-year period between 2001 and 2025:

  • The median age at death in Australia increased from 78.5 years to 81.9 years.
  • For males, the median age at death increased from 75.5 years to 79.7 years, with 2025 recording the equal highest median age at death for males in the time series (equal with 2022). 
  • For females, the median age at death increased from 81.8 years to 84.6 years.
  • The difference between the national median age at death for males and females has narrowed. 2018 was the first year in which the median age for males and females differed by less than six years. In 2025, the difference was less than 5 years for the first time.
  • Growth in median age has slowed. From 2000 to 2005, median age at death increased by an average of 0.32 years of age annually. From 2020 to 2025, the average annual increase in median age at death was 0.06 years.

Historical trends in median age at death

In 2025:

  • The median age at death for ischaemic heart diseases was 82.6 years, which is the lowest recorded since 2004 (82.4 years).
  •  The median age at death for lung cancer was the highest in the 25-year time series at 75.4 years.

Between 2001 and 2025:

  • For deaths due to dementia, the median age at death increased to a peak of 88.9 years in 2020 and 2021.
  • The highest recorded median age at death for ischaemic heart diseases was 85.0 years in 2015 and 2016. From 2017 to 2025, the median age at death decreased by an average of 0.27 years of age annually.
  • For deaths due to cerebrovascular diseases and chronic lower respiratory diseases, the median age at death also peaked in the mid-2010s (cerebrovascular diseases in 2015 at 86.6 years and chronic lower respiratory diseases in 2016 at 81.8 years). The median age at death has since been trending downward over time for both these causes of death.
  • From 2001 to 2018, there was less than one year separating the median age at death for chronic lower respiratory diseases and the median age at death for all causes of death. This gap has since widened and in 2025, the median age of those who died from chronic lower respiratory diseases was 1.7 years lower than the all-cause median.

Median age at death by socio-economic index

Median age at death also varies by socio-economic index. The Socio-Economic Index for Areas: Index of Relative Socio-economic Advantage and Disadvantage (SEIFA IRSAD) summarises information about the economic and social conditions of people and households within an area. This index includes both relative advantage and disadvantage measures. A low score indicates relatively greater disadvantage and a lack of advantage in general, whilst a high score indicates a relative lack of disadvantage and greater advantage in general (for more information, see Socio-Economic Indexes for Areas).

In 2025:

  • Increased socio-economic advantage (and relative lack of disadvantage) was generally associated with higher median age at death.
  • For the causes listed below, the median age at death for those in Australia's most advantaged areas (quintile 5) was on average 2.7 years higher than the median age at death for those in its least advantaged areas (quintile 1).
  • Quintile 5 (most advantaged, least disadvantaged) had the highest median age at death for 9 of the top 10 leading causes of death.
  • Quintile 1 (least advantaged, most disadvantaged) had the lowest median age at death for 9 of the top 10 leading causes of death.

Leading causes of death by sex

In 2025:

  • For males, the leading cause of death was ischaemic heart diseases (354 deaths) and the second leading cause was chronic lower respiratory diseases (230 deaths). For the first time since 2012, intentional self-harm (196 deaths) has moved from the second to the third leading cause of death.
  • For males, chronic lower respiratory diseases had the largest increase between 2024 and 2025, with an additional 48 deaths.
  • For females, the leading cause of death was diabetes (199 deaths), followed by ischaemic heart disease (189 deaths). 
  • Diabetes moved from the second leading cause of death in 2024 for females to the leading cause of death in 2025. For all persons, diabetes remained the third leading cause of death in 2025. 
  • Influenza and pneumonia deaths have increased by from 87 to 104 deaths between 2024 and 2025.

Between 2016 and 2025:

  • The top 5 leading causes remained the same across the period (ischaemic heart diseases, diabetes, chronic lower respiratory diseases, lung cancer and intentional self-harm) with some changes in rankings. The top 5 leading causes of death accounted for over one-third of all deaths.
  • The age-standardised death rate for diabetes has decreased over the ten-year time series.

Leading causes of death by remoteness

The tables below show the number of deaths and age-standardised death rates for the top 5 leading causes of death in for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people living in Australia's Major Cities, Inner and Outer Regional areas, and Remote and Very Remote areas. Classes of remoteness are characterised by a measure of relative geographic access to services (for more information, see Remoteness Areas). Remoteness data is based on area of usual residence aligned to the 2021 Australian Statistical Geography Standard (ASGS).

In 2025:

  • Ischaemic heart diseases were the leading cause of death for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people across all remoteness areas.
  • Chronic lower respiratory diseases were the second leading cause of death for those in major cities and regional Australia. 
  • Diabetes was the second leading cause of death for those in remote and very remote Australia, with an age-standardised death rate of 133.0 compared with 37.5 in major cities and 56.2 in regional areas.
  • Diabetes was not in the five leading causes of death for those in major cities, where it was ranked 7th in 2025 with 88 deaths. Accidental poisoning was the 5th leading cause in major cities.
  • The age-standardised death rate for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people in remote and very remote Australia was 18.6% higher than the rate in major cities in 2025. The difference in the death rates in remote and very remote Australia compared with major cities has been reducing over the last 5 years.

Methodology

Scope

All deaths that occurred and were registered in Australia, including deaths of persons whose place of usual residence was overseas.

Geography

Usual residence data is coded to the Australian Statistical Geography Standard (ASGS) 2021 – Edition 3.

Source

Death registrations from the state and territory Registries of Births, Deaths and Marriages (RBDMs). For deaths certified by a coroner, information is supplemented from the National Coronial Information System (NCIS).

Collection method

Administrative data from:

  • Death registration statements via funeral directors with family or acquaintances.
  • Medical Certificates of Cause of Death for doctor-certified deaths and various coronial reports for coroner-certified deaths.

Concepts, sources and methods

Cause of death information is coded to the 10th revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10). 

Descriptions of the underlying concepts and methods used are available in the methodology.

History of changes

Not applicable to this release.
